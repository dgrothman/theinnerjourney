# Class 3 · Boundaries — Teaching Notes

**Teaching hour:** 60 min · **Group hour:** follows
**Big idea:** Boundaries are the fences that contain and protect our reality. They protect us from others, and others from us.
**Bring:** Class 3 handout (exercise + boundary statements), pens.
**Builds on:** Class 2 (Reality). A boundary only makes sense once we know what our reality is.

## Head: What & Why (15 min)

### Slide 4 — What are they?
- Definition, word for word: *"A system of limit setting that protects a person from being a victim and contains a person so that they are not offensive to others."* (old notes, after Pia Mellody)
- Three purposes of the fence:
  - keep others from coming into our reality and abusing us
  - keep us from going into someone else's reality and abusing them
  - give us a way to embody our sense of "who we are"
- Most people just want to be allowed to be who they are. Being affirmed is good, as long as no boundary is being violated.

### Slide 5 — External boundaries
- Physical: how close people come, and whether they can touch us. Intact external boundaries also mean we ask before touching others.
- Sexual: the same for sexual distance and touch.
- From the old notes: start with the external physical boundary. The internal boundary won't work well if this one isn't in place.

### Slide 6 — Internal boundaries
- Contain our thinking, feelings and behavior so we take responsibility for them instead of blaming others.
- Release responsibility for other people's reality. This is what stops negative control, projection and manipulation.
- Key line: *"I create what I think and feel, and I am in control of what I do and don't do."*
- Meaning-making: we often blame people for a feeling that actually comes from the meaning *we* assigned, based on our belief system. (old notes)

### Slide 7 — Myths about boundaries
- Walk the three myths: selfish/disobedient (guilt, shame); I'll hurt others or get hurt; they're permanent.
- From *Common Myths About Boundaries*: appropriate boundaries *increase* our ability to care. People with shaky limits are often compliant on the outside and resentful on the inside.
- Permanent? No. Boundaries are a door we open and close, not a wall. (Previews slide 17.)

### Slide 8 — "Resentment is always a boundary issue."
- Pause here. Let it land, then ask the room: "Where does resentment show up for you?" Take one or two answers, no discussion.
- Say: "Hold onto that. It comes back in group time."

### Slide 9 — Boundary violations
- External: someone's space or belongings without permission (unwanted touch, going through drawers, checking a phone), listening in without permission.
- Internal: yelling, name calling, ridiculing, shaming or patronizing, and negative control: telling someone how they should feel, think or act.
- Connect to Class 2: negative control is crossing someone's internal boundary.

### Slide 10 — Boundaries are personal
- Everyone's will look different. Four questions to find yours:
  - What hurts? (how do I stop the pain?)
  - What feels good? (pursuing joy)
  - What is mine? (knowing my reality)
  - What am I willing to lose or give up?

## Hand: Motivation (6 min)

### Slide 11 — Barriers to boundaries
- Belief system: history and distorted thinking. When our reality is skewed, boundaries are hard to set.
- Enabling: a destructive form of helping. We attach to someone's need for us, then feel used.
- Enmeshment: so involved in someone else's problems that we detach from ourselves.

### Slide 12 — When we don't have boundaries
- Avoidance/procrastination, self-sabotage ("self-fulfilling the lie"), drama (getting the focus onto someone else), self-medicating.
- Ask: "Which of these is your go-to?" Rhetorical; no answers needed.

## Heart: Tools (29 min)

### Slide 13 — Tips for setting boundaries
- Know your reality first (self-awareness, define it). Then: say "no", set limits, practice self-control, set goals and be patient.
- Putting boundaries into words, two example lines:
  - *"I'd like to revisit this later, when the energy has dissipated."*
  - *"I hear that you think I am ___. I'd like to think about this, because I'm not sure what to do with it."*

### Slide 14 — Boundaries and relationships (source)
- Next section draws on *Maybe You Should Talk to Someone* by Lori Gottlieb.

### Slide 15 — Truths about boundaries in relationships
- We're responsible for knowing our impact on others. Be sensitive, but don't control.
- When we're pressured, managing our reaction is our job. Let go of the expectation and communicate.
- We *decide* to adjust or give something up. We don't mindlessly lose our reality to another person.
- Openness plus boundaries leads to connection and intimacy.
- From the old notes, word for word: *"I need to note the impact I have on others, and they need to note the impact they have on me."* Noting impact means active listening. If we stop listening, the boundary becomes a wall.

### Slide 16 — Boundary sketches
- Introduce the four kinds: healthy, none, partial, walls. Point to the sketches on the handout.

### Slide 17 — Healthy boundaries
- A door with the handle on the inside. We choose when to open and let someone in (connection) and when to keep it closed (protection, both ways).

### Slide 18 — No boundaries
- We don't know when we're being abused, or abusing. Trouble saying no; easily taken advantage of.
- From *Boundary Sketch*: people with no boundaries also walk through other people's boundaries without noticing.

### Slide 19 — Partial boundaries
- Can set limits with some people or at some times, not others. Common example: fine with everyone except a spouse, a parent or a boss.

### Slide 20 — Walls
- Walls give solid protection but no connection, so they isolate. People move from one wall to another.
- Anger (erupting volcano), fear (retreat to stay safe), silence (emotions off, the silent treatment), words (talking over everyone).

### Slide 22 — Exercise: Where are my boundaries? (10 min)
- Run-sheet:
  1. Handouts out. Answer the four yes/no questions alone.
  2. Mark which boundary type (healthy, none, partial, wall) you use most with family, work, friends and partner.
  3. 5 minutes writing, then 3 minutes sharing with one partner.
  4. Debrief (2 min): "Did anyone's boundary type change depending on the relationship?" Partial boundaries are the most common answer; normalize it.
- Remind people they can pass on sharing.

### Slide 23 — Take-home: My boundary statements
- Pick one boundary statement from the handout to practice this week. Decide when and with whom.
- It counts even if you only *think* it. Notice how it feels.
- Bring what you noticed to next week.
- ⚑ Review: from the old notes, if someone has built a wall because of abuse, that boundary type may need therapy first. Consider saying this gently here, and pointing to counseling resources.
