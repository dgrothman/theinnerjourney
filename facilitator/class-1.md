# Class 1 · Safe People & Feelings — Teaching Notes

**Teaching hour:** 60 min · **Group hour:** follows
**Big idea:** We heal in safe relationships, and healing starts with knowing who is safe, understanding how people are wired, and learning to name and work with what we feel.
**Bring:** Class 1 handouts (Safe people check, ANT log), release papers, pens.
**Builds on:** Nothing. This is the first class.

## Head: What & Why (27 min)

### Slide 5 — Safe People
- Section title. Content in this section is from *Hiding From Love* by Dr. John Townsend.
- Say: "Learning that we have the ability and control over who we share ourselves with is a great first step in our growth." (old notes)
- Ask: "What are some ways we can identify people who are safe and can help us on this journey?" (old notes) Take one or two answers, then go to the next slide.

### Slide 6 — Safe People Are (1 of 3)
- Walk the three traits with the explanations on the slide (also in *Safe People Are*):
  - People who react to you in a different way than those who injured you. Use your memory to tell the difference.
  - People who, over time, have a loving track record. See if their walk matches their talk.
  - People who can be observed from some emotional distance. Take small risks in vulnerability before taking bigger risks.

### Slide 7 — Safe People Are (2 of 3)
- People with the ability to accept imperfections in others: the difference between those who love the outside and those who love the inner self.
- People who are no stranger to pain, yet are recovering. Those who have suffered spiritually and emotionally can identify with developmental injuries.
- People who are aware of their own deficits. Those who know their unfinished parts are more likely to be safe with the unfinished parts of others.

### Slide 8 — Safe People Are (3 of 3)
- People who have truth without condemnation. It's easier to entrust our weaknesses to those who love us and who will speak the truth in love.
- People who have grace without license. Attaching to others who see grace as leading to greater responsibility helps us experience grace appropriately.
- People who bring out your best in life. Ask: has this relationship made me more or less loving and responsible? (*Safe People Are* words this one "bear good fruit in your life.")
- From the article: break the dysfunctional family rules of "don't talk, don't trust, don't feel" by seeking a confessional relationship. A safe person will give you permission to be human, even when you can't give it to yourself. (*Safe People Are*)

### Slide 13 — The Four Color Code Personalities
- Section title: Blue, Red, White, Yellow. Content in this section is from colorcode.com.
- Say: "We all have a personality, a bias or tint on how we interpret life around us and our relationships. Understanding different personalities allows us to not take things so personally, to understand where people are coming from, and to understand some of our triggers and default reactions." (old notes)
- ⚑ Review: Tell the room most of us are a blend, and the point is understanding, not putting people in boxes.

### Slide 14 — Blue Personality Strengths
- Sees life as a serious endeavor; sincere and emotionally deep; analytically oriented; high achiever.
- *Color Code: Blue* adds: appreciates beauty and detail, strong aesthetic sense, deep sense of purpose.

### Slide 15 — Blue Personality Limitations
- Highly emotional; smug and self-righteous; controlling and/or envious of others' success when too easily obtained; strong perfecting and performance orientation.
- Connecting well with Blue (*Color Code: Blue*): emphasize their security, be sincere, give them time to gather their thoughts. Don't make them feel guilty, demand perfection (they already expect too much of themselves) or abandon them.

### Slide 16 — Red Personality Strengths
- Excels with logical thinking; committed to a productive lifestyle; dynamic and direct; thrives on independence; natural leader; highly resourceful (a strong survivor); creative in crisis.

### Slide 17 — Red Personality Limitations
- Promotes turmoil and conflict when a personal goal is to be gained; rationalizes and denies own failings; always right; often arrogant and defiant of authority; impatient with others.
- ⚑ Review: The slide has typos ("Renationalizes", "defiant or authority", "Inpatient"). The article has the same ones. Say the corrected words aloud and fix the deck.
- Connecting well with Red (*Color Code: Red*): be direct, brief and specific; present issues logically; respect their need to make decisions their own way. Don't embarrass them in front of others or argue from an emotional perspective.

### Slide 18 — White Personality Strengths
- Quiet, reflective and peaceful; sincere and genuine lifestyle; patient with others; blends into all situations.

### Slide 19 — White Personality Limitations
- Unresponsive or not openly excited about experiences; has problems becoming intimate; bashful and unsure of self; often lazy and unwilling to take responsibility for self; resists making commitments.
- Connecting well with White (*Color Code: White*): be kind, patient and gentle; offer options; accept written communication. Don't force immediate verbal responses or confrontation, and don't overwhelm them.

### Slide 20 — Yellow Personality Strengths
- Highly optimistic (rarely depressed); likes self and accepts others easily; loves to volunteer for opportunities; sees life as an experience to be enjoyed; adventurous and daring.

### Slide 21 — Yellow Personality Limitations
- Needs to look good socially (high priority); irresponsible and unreliable; self-centered and egotistical; superficial and mostly interested in a good time; exaggerates successes and omits unpleasant truths; unable to confront or face issues.
- Connecting well with Yellow (*Color Code: Yellow*): praise them legitimately, remember they are more sensitive than they appear, allow time for verbal expression. Don't be too serious in criticism or ignore them.
- ⚑ Review: Close the color code section with a show of hands for "which color do you think you lean toward?" Light and optional; people can pass.

### Slide 25 — Feelings chart
- Overview of feelings according to Pia Mellody (from the slide notes). Walk the chart left to right: each feeling has a gift (adult feeling reality) and a legacy of abuse (child feeling reality).

- Say: "Every feeling has a gift. When we've been hurt, we can carry the feeling in a bigger, heavier form from childhood."

### Slide 26 — What generates a feeling?
- Four steps (also *What Generates Feelings?*):
  - Body: our five senses bring input from the outer world to our inner world.
  - Thinking: we interpret, draw conclusions and give meaning to the input.
  - Feeling: the physiological response to our thoughts (also called emotions). It uses up energy in our body.
  - Behavior: what we choose to do, or not do, as a result of our feelings.
- Say: "Our feelings are created by our thoughts. And our behavior comes out of our feelings." (*Feelings Chart*)
- From the article: the experience of being abused damages our thinking. We assign meaning that is often inaccurate, and we don't know it.
- Example from the article: a genuine compliment gets turned into a subtle insult because we label it sarcasm, and we believe our thinking is fine. (*What Generates Feelings?*)

## Hand: Motivation (3 min)

### Slide 27 — Facts about feelings
- Our belief system affects the way we think, feel and behave. Thoughts generate feelings, which generate behavior.
- How we process our thoughts affects the feeling and its intensity: positive vs. negative thoughts.
- Say: "What we feel is what we feel. We cannot change our feelings, but we can change our thoughts to change our feelings." (slide; *What Generates Feelings?*)
- Why it matters:
  - Processing a feeling starts with acknowledging it.
  - Denied feelings are a trigger for inappropriate behavior.
  - Unprocessed feelings are stored in our body and carried with us.
- From the article: it is dysfunctional to try not to be angry or afraid when that is what we feel. (*What Generates Feelings?*)
- ⚑ Review: Ask, "Where in your body do you carry a feeling you haven't dealt with?" Rhetorical; no answers needed.

## Heart: Tools (14 min)

### Slide 28 — Tools for feelings
- Allow yourself to feel them. Let them be as big (or little), overwhelming or confusing (ugly) as they really are.
- Find the truth: What or who is the source, external or internal? Is there an event that triggered it?
- Use the True, Not True, Questionable Tool to filter out thoughts that don't belong.
  - ⚑ Review: no description of this tool exists in the sources. Explain it yourself (true: facts I can verify; not true: things I know are wrong; questionable: things I'm not sure of) or bring a handout.
- Talk them out with a safe person. This is where slides 6 to 10 pay off.
- Purge them: journal, writing, art; or physically (exercise, move, express physically).
- Express them in a way that does not harm you or others. From *How to Handle Anger*: assertive anger respects another person's dignity; aggressive anger does not.
- From *Stopping Our Pain*: busyness, food, money, drink and other ways of numbing are Band-Aids; they postpone the pain. We can face it and feel it.

### Slide 29 — Automatic Negative Thoughts
- Section title. Content in this section is from *Change Your Brain Change Your Life* by Daniel Amen.
- Say: "Negative thoughts invade your mind like ants at a picnic."

### Slide 30 — Examples of ANTs (1 of 3)
- When you notice an ANT, acknowledge it and talk back to it. When you hear yourself say one, stop and correct yourself.
- All or Nothing: everything is all good or all bad; black and white thinking. One event or moment doesn't define the whole.
  - Example from the article: miss one day at the gym and quit entirely. Better: acknowledge it, then get back on track the next day. (*Automatic Negative Thoughts*)
- Always Thinking: overgeneralizing with words like "always" or "never." Feels like you have no control over your behavior.
- Focusing on the Negative: only seeing the negative, regardless of the positives. Leads to hopelessness or wanting to give up.

### Slide 31 — Examples of ANTs (2 of 3)
- Thinking with Your Feelings: assuming a feeling is correct rather than questioning it. Feelings can lie, and our thoughts may not be correct.
- Guilt Beating: using guilt to control our behavior, or using negative control on others ("should," "must," "ought to," "have to"). When we feel pushed, our natural tendency is to push back.
- Labeling: calling yourself or another a negative name. Leads to worthlessness, helplessness and hopelessness; giving up before trying.

### Slide 32 — Red ANTs
- Beware of these three (*Automatic Negative Thoughts*):
  - Fortune-Telling: predicting the worst even though you don't know what will happen.
  - Mind Reading: assuming you know what another person is thinking, without asking.
  - Blame: blaming others and not taking responsibility for ourselves. It makes us feel powerless to change our own behavior.
- Say: "Of all the ANTs, Blame is the worst. When we blame, we make ourselves a victim, and victims are powerless to change." (*Automatic Negative Thoughts*)

### Slide 33 — Take-home: Catch an ANT
- Run-sheet for the week:
  1. Once a day, notice an automatic negative thought.
  2. Name its type: all or nothing, always thinking, focusing on the negative, thinking with your feelings, guilt beating, labeling, fortune-telling, mind reading, or blame.
  3. Talk back to it with a truer thought.
  4. Log it on your handout.
- ⚑ Review: Tell the room that noticing an ANT, even once all week, counts. Ask them to bring what they notice next week.
- ⚑ Review: The deck has no in-class practice for this. If there is time, have people name one ANT out loud and talk back to it together.
